Before the Lesson · Meet the Data

254,278 people.
Who are they?

Before we do any math, let's spend time with the people in this dataset. The U.S. Census Bureau counted every foreign-born resident in the Seattle area as part of the 2021 American Community Survey. That's over a quarter million people with distinct histories, languages, jobs, and reasons for coming to the U.S.

The survey breaks them into three groups — or cohorts — based on when they arrived:

Group A · Arrived 2010 or later

91,815 people. The most recent arrivals — some are only a few years into life in the U.S. Many are in their 20s and 30s. Some came for university or tech jobs. Others came as refugees. Their lives here are still taking shape.

Group B · Arrived 2000–2009

61,921 people. The "middle" cohort. They've had 12–21 years to settle in. Many have started families, built careers, and put down roots. This is the smallest of the three groups.

Group C · Arrived before 2000

100,542 people. The longest-established group — some have been here for 30, 40, even 50+ years. Many are now citizens, homeowners, and grandparents. Their median age is 53.9 years.

Where do they come from? Over half of all three groups were born in Asia — making Seattle's foreign-born population heavily Asian. But the composition shifts: Latin America's share triples from Group A (12.6%) to Group B (26.3%), while Africa's share more than doubles from Group C (6.5%) to Group A (15.5%). These patterns reflect how global migration to the U.S. has changed over decades.

Education

Group A stands out: 34.8% hold a graduate or professional degree, compared to 18.3% in Group B and 15.9% in Group C. Seattle's tech industry draws highly-educated workers from abroad. Housing & Economic Stability

Time in the country shows up clearly in housing. Only 22.2% of Group A own their home — nearly everyone rents. By Group C, that flips: 60.7% own. Buying a home takes years of savings and credit history. What might surprise you: Group A still has the highest median household income ($95,809) despite renting more and arriving most recently.

Citizenship & Language

Group C has the highest naturalization rate — 79% are citizens vs just 11.6% in Group A. Citizenship requires at minimum five years as a permanent resident, so Group A hasn't had time. On language: 82% of all foreign-born residents speak a language other than English at home, and around 42% across all three groups say they don't speak English "very well" — strikingly consistent across cohorts.

Why does Group A have the highest income AND the highest poverty rate at the same time? +
Group A's median household income is $95,809 — the highest of the three groups — yet it also has the highest poverty rate at 18.4%. The group is highly polarized. Seattle draws extremely high-earning tech workers and international students at one end, and refugees and low-wage service workers at the other. The median gets pulled up by high earners, but there's a large tail of people in poverty. This is exactly why single statistics can mislead — and it's central to what we'll practice in this lesson.
Group B has the highest labor force participation (79.2%) — why not Group A, the youngest group? +
Group B (arrived 2000–2009) has 79.2% of working-age adults in the labor force — higher than Group A (73.4%) and Group C (67.2%). Group B members arrived as working-age adults and are now in their prime earning years (roughly 38–55). Group C has more retirees, pulling their rate down. Group A includes more students and people still getting established. It's mostly a life-stage effect — not about motivation or opportunity.
Group A has more people than Group B — so does it always have more of everything? +
Not at all — and this is the core math insight for this lesson. Group A has 91,815 people, but only 22.2% own homes (~20,383 people). Group C has 100,542 people and 60.7% own homes (~61,029 people). Group C has roughly 3× as many home owners despite being only slightly bigger. Size matters, but rate matters more than you might expect.

Lesson · Comparing Percentages

When 57% and 41% tell completely different stories

Percentages are everywhere — in news headlines, government reports, your grade. But comparing percentages across different groups is where it gets interesting, and where people make the most mistakes.

In this lesson, you'll use real U.S. Census data about Seattle's immigrant population to practice comparing percentages the right way.

Notice anything? The groups have very different sizes. That's the first thing you always check when comparing percentages.

Part 1 · Quick Review

What a percentage actually means

A percentage is a way of expressing a number as a fraction of 100. It lets us compare things that come from groups of different sizes.

Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100 or: what fraction of the whole does the part represent?

For example: 57.2% of Group A (arrived 2010+) were born in Asia. That means:

57.2% of 91,815 ≈ 52,518 people born in Asia in Group A
Key Idea The same percentage means a different number of people depending on how big the group is. 57% of 91,815 is very different from 57% of 500.

Part 2 · Core Skill

How to compare percentages across groups

When comparing percentages, you can ask three questions:

Question 1 — Which group has more? Simple: which percentage is larger? e.g. 34.8% vs 18.3% vs 15.9% — Group A wins.
Question 2 — How much more? (Percentage point difference) Subtract one percentage from another. This gives you the gap. e.g. 34.8% − 15.9% = 18.9 percentage points difference between Group A and Group C.
Question 3 — How many times more? (Ratio) Divide one percentage by another. This shows relative size of the difference. e.g. 34.8 ÷ 15.9 ≈ 2.2× — Group A has more than twice the grad degree rate.

Part 3 · Watch Out

Three traps when comparing percentages

⚠ Trap 1 · Forgetting group size

"Group A and Group C both have ~57% from Asia, so they must have the same number of Asian-born residents." Wrong. Group C has ~100k total vs Group A's ~92k — so Group C actually has more people, even at the same percentage.

⚠ Trap 2 · Confusing percentage points with percent change

Group A has 34.8% with grad degrees. Group C has 15.9%. The difference is 18.9 percentage points — but as a percent change, Group A's rate is 119% higher than Group C's. Those are very different claims. Always be clear which one you mean.

⚠ Trap 3 · A higher % doesn't always mean "better"

Group A has the highest poverty rate (18.4%) AND the highest median household income ($95,809). How? Recent arrivals are diverse — some are highly-paid tech workers, others are recent refugees. One percentage can't tell the whole story.

The paradox: Group A has highest income AND highest poverty
Takeaway Always ask: What question am I actually trying to answer? One comparison in isolation can mislead. Look at multiple indicators before drawing conclusions.

Lesson Complete

What you learned

✓ Group size matters

The same percentage means different raw numbers depending on how big the group is. Always check the denominator.

✓ Percentage point difference

Subtract percentages to find the gap. "34.8% vs 15.9%" → 18.9 pp difference. This is not the same as percent change.

✓ Ratios show relative size

Dividing percentages tells you how many times larger one rate is. 34.8 ÷ 15.9 ≈ 2.2× — a more intuitive comparison.

✓ No single percentage tells the whole story

Group A had the highest income AND poverty rate — seeming contradictions that reveal real complexity in the data.

Real-World Skill Every time you see a headline like "Group X is 2× more likely to…" — you now know to ask: 2× more likely than what? How big are the groups? Is that percentage points or a ratio? You're reading data more critically than most adults.
